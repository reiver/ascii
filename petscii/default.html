PETSCII

the character-set of Commodore's 8-bit computers

by Charles Iliya Krempeaux

PETSCII is the character-set used by Commodore's 8-bit home computers — the PET (1977), the VIC-20, the Commodore 64, the Commodore 16 and Plus/4, and the Commodore 128.

Commodore

I used a Commodore 64 computer when I was a kid in the 1980s — at the home of a neighbor. And thus, I used PETSCII. I remember spending a lot of time at this neighbor's home, usually playing video-games on their Commodore 64 — and sometimes (when they let us) jumping on their trampoline.

There is an important era of the history of computing that we could accurately call the Commodore era. You can probably consider the years from about 1977 to 1994 as being the Commodore era, with the golden-age of the Commodore era being from about the years 1980 to 1989.

Commodore was a big deal during this era, especially the Commodore 64 computer. As big of a deal (in the the history of computing) as the Apple iPhone, the Nintendo NES, and the Atari 2600.

I was a kid during this era, but I remember it. I am a child of the mid- 1970s and 1980s mostly living in various suburbs of the city of Vancouver on the west coast of Canada.

Families had electronics in the 1980s, but they weren't as common or as ubiquitous as they are in the 2020s. Most families in my part of the world:

  • had a TV — either a black-and-white TV or a color TV
  • had radios — often both in their home, and built into their vehcile (in their car)

Some families in my part of the world:

  • had calculators

For most families in my part of the world, that might be it in terms of electronics.

During that era, it was rare for people to have a computer with a keyboard in their home. Some families had a computer without a keyboard in this homes, but even that was a minority. Your school might have a small number of computers with keyboards. But, if your family was fortunate enough to have a computer at home, it likely didn't have a keyboard. My family had an Atari 2600 (Atari VCS). One of my friend's family had a ColecoVision. I had seen the Atari 5200, Intellivision, and Vectrex in store (probably at Kmart), and played them in store, but I don't recall any of my friend's families having one. Most of my friends' families had none of these! One family in the neighborhood I lived in (in the city of Port Coquitlam) had a Commodore 64. From the perspective of a kid, it was different than the Atari 2600, the Atari 5200, ColecoVision, Intellivision, and Vectrex, because the Commodore 64 had a keyboard. That family (the mother really) was kind) and would let the kids in the neighborhood (who were friends with her kids) play on the Commodore 64. The Commodore 64 was a computer with a keyboard, but back then (when I was still in elementary school) what I recall mostly doing on it is playing video-games.

PETSCII is based on ASCII — but on the 1963 version of ASCII, rather than the version of ASCII used today. And, PETSCII adds a large number of graphics-characters (lines, corners, blocks, card suits, and more) that can be typed straight from the keyboard and used to draw pictures.

Although PETSCII looks a lot like ASCII, it is not compatible with any version of ASCII. Its control-codes are almost all different. And, in one of its two modes, its upper-case and lower-case letters are swapped compared to ASCII.

(The name “PETSCII” does not seem to come from Commodore. Commodore's own manuals called it “ASCII”, “Commodore ASCII”, “CBM ASCII”, or “PET ASCII”. My guess is that, “PETSCII” may have been a contraction of “PET ASCII”.)

Origins

The Commodore PET 2001 was one of the first personal computers — sold ready to use, with its keyboard, screen, and cassette-tape drive (for storing programs) all built into a single case. Along with the Apple II and the TRS-80 (both also from 1977), it is often counted among the first successful mass-produced personal computers. It sold for $595 (with 4 kilobytes of memory) or $795 (with 8 kilobytes of memory). (“PET” stands for Personal Electronic Transactor.)

The PET 2001 was designed by a team led by Chuck Peddle. It was previewed at the Consumer Electronics Show in January 1977, and the first 100 units shipped in mid-October 1977.

Its character-set was designed by Leonard Tramiel (son of Commodore's founder, Jack Tramiel), working to Peddle's requirements. Tramiel later wrote, about Peddle:

He gave me the task of designing that character set.

The one firm requirement was the four card suits — ♠, ♥, ♦, and ♣ — because card games, such as Blackjack, were expected to be popular on the machine.

Tramiel also included every possible 2×2 pattern of quarter-blocks (only 8 characters are needed, since reverse-video provides the other 8). With these, the PET's 40×25 text screen could be used as an 80×50 pseudo-graphics screen. (See “Pseudo-Graphics”, below.)

The graphics-characters were arranged so that each one is typed by holding shift and pressing a normal key. An early review of the PET, in BYTE magazine (March 1978), said that the PET was:

better adapted for drawing pictures of this sort than for plotting mathematical functions.

(Tramiel also remembers including a £ (pound sign). But, the PET's character ROMs have \ (backslash) in that position. The £ first appeared with the VIC-20.)

Based on ASCII 1963

PETSCII follows the 1963 version of ASCII — which had ↑ (up-arrow) and ← (left-arrow) where ASCII today has ^ and _. (See the history of ASCII.)

Why the 1963 version, and not the 1967 version? That does not seem to be documented.

Codes 0x20 to 0x5B, and 0x5D, are the same as ASCII — space, punctuation, Arabic-numerals, @, the letters A to Z (in the default mode), [, and ]. But, PETSCII has no `, {, |, }, or ~ anywhere.

Two Modes

PETSCII has two modes, each with its own set of characters:

  • upper-case / graphics mode (the default) — upper-case letters, plus the full set of graphics-characters,
  • lower-case / upper-case mode (sometimes called text or business mode) — lower-case letters, upper-case letters, and fewer graphics-characters.

Switching modes does not change any of the codes. It changes how the codes are displayed.

Switching Modes

On the Commodore 64, the mode can be switched by:

  • pressing shift and the Commodore key together,
  • printing CHR$(14) (lower-case / upper-case) or CHR$(142) (upper-case / graphics),
  • or, POKE 53272,23 (lower-case / upper-case) or POKE 53272,21 (upper-case / graphics).

And, printing CHR$(8) disables switching with shift and Commodore, and CHR$(9) enables it again.

On the original PET, the only way to switch was POKE 59468,14 (lower-case / upper-case) or POKE 59468,12 (upper-case / graphics).

The Original PET

On the original PET 2001, lower-case mode worked the other way around. Unshifted keys gave upper-case letters, and shifted keys gave lower-case letters. As BYTE magazine put it (March 1978):

(Yes, you must press the shift key to get lower case, and omit the shift key to get upper case!)

Later PETs swapped this. But, the PETSCII codes did not change — the character ROM did. The upper-case and lower-case pictures were swapped in the ROM. So, text written on an original PET shows up with its case reversed on a later one.

Control-Codes

On the PET 2001, VIC-20, and Commodore 64, only one PETSCII control-code means the same thing as in ASCII: RETURN (0x0D, which is CR in ASCII). The rest are Commodore's own — mostly for moving the cursor, editing the screen, and (on the VIC-20 and Commodore 64) changing color. (Later PETs, and the Commodore 128, added a few more that match ASCII — such as bell (0x07), tab (0x09), and ESC (0x1B).)

Many of them come in pairs, where the code of the second is the code of the first plus 0x80 (i.e., with bit-7 set) — often typed by the same key, with shift:

Notice that some of these clash with ASCII. For example, in ASCII, 0x11 and 0x13 are DC1 and DC3 — which are often used as XON and XOFF (to start and stop the flow of data). In PETSCII, they are cursor down and HOME. And, PETSCII's delete is 0x14 — while ASCII's backspace is 0x08.

Control-Codes in Strings

In Commodore BASIC, control-codes could be typed directly into a string, inside quotes. They would show up on the screen as reverse-video symbols — and then take effect when the string was printed. That is how many Commodore programs move the cursor and change colors.

Graphics-Characters

In upper-case / graphics mode, codes 0xA0 to 0xBF and 0xC0 to 0xDF are graphics-characters. These include:

  • horizontal, vertical, and diagonal lines,
  • corners, rounded corners, and line junctions — for drawing boxes,
  • half-blocks, quarter-blocks, and one-eighth blocks,
  • a checkerboard pattern,
  • triangles,
  • the card suits — ♠ (0xC1), ♥ (0xD3), ♣ (0xD8), and ♦ (0xDA),
  • a filled circle ● (0xD1) and an empty circle ○ (0xD7),
  • and π (0xDE, and also 0xFF — and, on the VIC-20 and Commodore 64, 0x7E).

In lower-case / upper-case mode, the codes 0xC1 to 0xDA become the upper-case letters — so the card suits (among others) are not available in that mode. Tramiel later wrote that he wished he had planned for lower-case earlier:

In particular I wish the card suits were still there with the lower case characters.

Duplicate Codes

Some PETSCII codes display the same character as other codes. The Commodore 64 Programmer's Reference Guide lists these:

CODES 192-223 SAME AS 96-127
CODES 224-254 SAME AS 160-190
CODE 255 SAME AS 126

But, the PET was different. On the PET, codes 0x60 to 0x7F repeat codes 0x20 to 0x3F instead. So, for example, on a PET CHR$(108) prints a comma.

The Commodore 64 Font

The Commodore 64 kept the same characters as the VIC-20, but made their lines thicker — so that every vertical line is at least 2 pixels wide. This avoided color errors on television screens.

Pseudo-Graphics

PETSCII includes characters that split a character cell into 4 quarters — 2×2 “pixels”. So, with these, the PET's 40×25 text screen can be used as an 80×50 pseudo-graphics screen.

There are 16 possible patterns of 4 quarters. But, PETSCII only needs 8 of them as characters — because reverse-video turns each of those 8 into one of the other 8. All 16 are in Unicode (in the Block Elements block).

Reverse-video flips every quarter — filled becomes empty, and empty becomes filled. Screen Codes

PETSCII codes are what programs print. But, what is actually stored in the screen's memory is a different numbering, called screen codes (or POKE codes).

Talking to ASCII

Since PETSCII is not ASCII, Commodore computers had to translate when exchanging text with other computers — for example, over a modem. The Commodore 64 Programmer's Reference Guide says:

When communicating with other computers, the Commodore character codes must be translated into their ASCII counterparts.

Without translation, text written in lower-case / upper-case mode comes out with its case wrong: lower-case letters (0x41 to 0x5A) arrive as ASCII upper-case letters, and upper-case letters (0xC1 to 0xDA) are not ASCII at all. Going the other way, ASCII lower-case letters (0x61 to 0x7A) show up on a Commodore 64 as graphics-characters.

Commodore's own printers did not use ASCII either. For example, on a Commodore printer, 14 means begin double-width (on the screen, it switches to lower-case / upper-case mode), and 17 switches the printer to lower-case / upper-case (on the screen, it moves the cursor down).

PETSCII Today

Unicode

Many PETSCII characters were already in Unicode — such as £ (U+00A3), π (U+03C0), ↑ (U+2191), ← (U+2190), the card suits, and many box-drawing characters and block elements. But, many were not.

In April 2017, a mailing-list discussion about PETSCII led to the formation of a Terminals Working Group. Its proposal — “Proposal to add characters from legacy computers and teletext to the UCS” (by Doug Ewell, Rebecca Bettencourt, and others) — begins:

A list discussion in April 2017 concerning the “PETSCII” character set, used in various forms by Commodore home computers ranging from the PET (1977) to the C128 (1985), led to the formation of an ad-hoc Terminals Working Group.

The result was the Symbols for Legacy Computing block (U+1FB00 to U+1FBFF), added in Unicode 13.0 on March 10th, 2020. It includes the remaining PETSCII graphics-characters — such as the one-eighth blocks, and the checkerboards (U+1FB95 for the PET and VIC-20, and U+1FB96 for the Commodore 64 and 128, which have it inverted).

So, today, every printable PETSCII character of the PET, VIC-20, Commodore 64, and Commodore 128 has a Unicode equivalent. But, two things were left out on purpose: reverse-video characters (which are considered a matter of display, not of character), and PETSCII's control-codes.

(See “Unicode Equivalents”, below, for every PETSCII character and its Unicode equivalent.)

PETSCII Art

Because its graphics-characters can be typed straight from the keyboard, PETSCII has always been used to make pictures — in early games, in Commodore BBS menus and logos, and in the demoscene.

Since around 2013, there has been a revival of PETSCII art. The Plain PETSCII Graphics Competition in 2013 received 106 entries, with the rules requiring an unmodified Commodore 64 and its built-in characters only. Demoparties have since held PETSCII competitions. And, there are modern PETSCII editors — such as Marq's PETSCII editor, Petmate, and Playscii.

A 2018 academic paper, “PETSCII – A Character Set and a Creative Platform” (by Markku Reunanen, Tero Heikkinen, and Anders Carlsson), studied this — and describes PETSCII as a low-threshold way into the Commodore 64 scene.

BBSes

Commodore BBSes used PETSCII — including its colors and graphics-characters — in what is usually called C/G (color / graphics) mode. Some PETSCII BBSes are still running today, reachable over telnet.

New Machines

PETSCII is still used by new 8-bit computers inspired by Commodore's. The MEGA65 uses the Commodore 64's PETSCII characters. And, the Commander X16 uses PETSCII by default — but also has an ISO mode (using ISO-8859-15), which is much closer to ASCII.

(Commodore's later Amiga computers did not use PETSCII. They used ISO 8859-1 (Latin-1), which contains ASCII.)

  • the mapping tables map 0xFF to U+259A (quadrant upper left and lower right), as a repeat of 0xBF — but, the Commodore 64 Programmer's Reference Guide says “CODE 255 SAME AS 126”, and so the table shows 0xFF the same as 0x7E and 0xDE.

Also, the PET differences in the table are for later PETs. On the original PET 2001, in lower-case / upper-case mode, 0x41 to 0x5A displayed upper-case letters, and 0xC1 to 0xDA displayed lower-case letters (see “The Original PET”, above).

And, reverse-video is not in the table — since reverse-video is part of the screen codes, not the PETSCII codes.

Control-Codes

Unicode does not have PETSCII's control-codes. By convention, PETSCII's control-codes are mapped to the Unicode control-codes with the same number — i.e., 0x00 to 0x1F map to U+0000 to U+001F, and 0x80 to 0x9F map to U+0080 to U+009F.

Note that this keeps the number, not the meaning. For example, PETSCII's cursor down (0x11) maps to U+0011 — which, in Unicode (and ASCII), is DC1.
