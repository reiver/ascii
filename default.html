ASCII

a popular character encoding

by Charles Iliya Krempeaux

ASCII (short for American Standard Code for Information Interchange) is a historically important character-set that billions of people have used either on its own or embedded in other character-sets such as CP437 (code page 437), PETSCII, Unicode, and others.

ASCII includes symbols for the upper-case (A-Z) and lower-case (a-z) versions of the English-language alphabet, symbols for Arabic-numerals (0-9), symbols for many English-language punctuation marks, symbols for some diacritical marks, and control-codes.

ASCII has had a large impact on computing, on computer-technologies, on computer-software, on computer-hardware, on computer-networks, on the Internet, on the Web, and on human-cultures that interacted with any of these. Of course, ASCII was heavily influenced by things that existed during and before its creation — and thus, ASCII's influence on humanity was part of broader phenomena.

As of the year 2025, the ASCII character-set is still in use as part of the Unicode character-set. And, often by itself, for many Internet computer-protocols.

128 Characters

ASCII has 128 characters — numbered 0 to 127.

Although many (probably most) people who have used ASCII have used it as part of character-sets with more-than 128 characters (such as CP437 (code page 437), PETSCII, and Unicode), ASCII itself has only 128 characters.

This pattern is true for every letter in ASCII.

This means that a number of bit-tricks can be used to do things such as:

  • compare letters in a case-insensitive manner,
  • lower-case letters,
  • upper-case letters,
  • toggle between upper-case and lower-case (and vice-versa),
  • etc.

All of these bit-tricks work by changing only bit-5. Bit-5 has the value 0x20 (binary 0b0100000).

They use three bitwise-operations, which work on each bit separately:

  • bitwise-OR (|) — sets a bit to 1 wherever the mask has a 1,
  • bitwise-AND (&) — clears a bit to 0 wherever the mask has a 0,
  • bitwise-XOR (^) — flips a bit wherever the mask has a 1.

Bits where the mask does not say to change anything are left alone.

Note that these bit-tricks only work on letters. Used on other characters, they can turn one character into a different, unrelated character. For example, “@” (0x40) bitwise-OR 0x20 gives “`” (0x60), and “[” (0x5B) bitwise-OR 0x20 gives “{” (0x7B). That also means that a case-insensitive comparison done this way would say that “@” and “`” are equal.

So, you would need to check that a character is a letter (A to Z, or a to z) before using these bit-tricks on it.

History

ASCII was designed by a committee between 1960 and 1963, and revised several times after that — with the version most people know today being (more or less) settled by 1967.

For more, see the history of ASCII.
