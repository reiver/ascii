History of ASCII

how a 7-bit code came to be, and how it spread

by Charles Iliya Krempeaux

ASCII was not invented all at once, by one person, out of nothing. It was designed by a committee, between 1960 and 1963, to fix problems that had built up over almost 100 years of telegraph-codes, teleprinter-codes, and early computer-codes. And, after its first edition in 1963, it was revised several times — with the version most people know today being (more or less) settled by 1967.

This article goes through that history: what came before ASCII, how ASCII was designed (and why it looks the way it does), how it changed, how it spread, and how it lives on today inside Unicode and UTF-8.

(For an explanation of the ASCII character-set itself, see ASCII.)

Before ASCII

Telegraph-Codes

In the 1870s, Émile Baudot, of the French telegraph administration, created a printing-telegraph that used a 5-unit code. 5 units gives only 32 combinations — which is not enough for letters and numerals. So, Baudot's code used two special characters — a letter-shift and a figure-shift — to switch between two meanings for most of the other codes.

Around 1900, Donald Murray created a 5-unit code for a typewriter-like keyboard that punched paper-tape. Murray arranged his code so that the most frequent letters punched the fewest holes (to reduce wear on the machinery). And, by 1905, the all-holes-punched code had become a non-printing character — so that a typing mistake on the paper-tape could be “erased” by punching every hole over it. This is the ancestor of ASCII's DEL character.

Murray's code became the basis of ITA2 (International Telegraph Alphabet No. 2), which the CCIT standardized in 1931. ITA2 was used by teleprinters around the world for decades. (ITA2 is often called “Baudot code”, but it descends mostly from Murray's code.)

But, these 5-unit codes had problems for computers:

  • they were shifted codes — the meaning of a code depended on whichever shift character came before it, so one lost shift character could garble everything after it,
  • the letters were not in alphabetical order, and the numerals were not in numeric order — so sorting by binary value did not work,
  • they had very few control-codes.

Computer-Codes

Early computers did not use telegraph-codes. Each computer-maker created its own codes — often more than one.

IBM, for example, used 6-bit BCD codes derived from its punched-card code. And, the US Army Signal Corps created FIELDATA (around 1959–1960), a code for military communications.

By 1960, there were dozens of incompatible character-codes in use. Minutes from a 1960 industry committee counted at least 29. Moving data from one manufacturer's equipment to another's meant translating it.

The Committee

In 1960, the ASA (American Standards Association) set up a committee for computers and information processing, named X3. X3 was organized on January 13th, 1960, and held its first meeting on August 4th, 1960. At that meeting it created a subcommittee for character-codes, named X3.2.

X3.2 first met on October 6th, 1960. This is the date generally given as the start of the work on ASCII.

X3.2's members came from the companies and organizations that made, or used, communication and computing equipment — including AT&T, Bell Telephone Laboratories, Teletype Corporation, IBM, Burroughs, Sperry Rand, NCR, RCA, Honeywell, and the US Department of Defense. Its first chairman was I. C. Liggett, of IBM. By the time the standard was published, the chairman was C. E. Macon, of Burroughs.

AT&T had a practical reason to be involved. It was converting its TWX (Teletypewriter Exchange) network to dial service, and needed new teleprinters. That was a chance to leave the 5-unit codes behind.

There was also coordination with Europe. Hugh McGregor Ross, of Ferranti in the UK, worked on a British code through the BSI (British Standards Institution), and on European and international codes. In Ross's code, the alphabet started at the second position of its column — as in most British 5-track computer codes. ASCII took this layout — which is why A is 0x41 (rather than 0x40).

Bob Bemer

Bob Bemer, of IBM, is sometimes called the “father of ASCII”. He did make real contributions:

  • he was involved in getting the X3 committee started,
  • he co-authored a code proposal that X3.2 discussed in March 1961,
  • his 1961 letter proposed the backslash (\) and square brackets ([ and ]) that were adopted,
  • he promoted the idea of an escape character (ESC).

But, ASCII was the work of a committee. Bemer was not even on the list of X3.2 members printed in the 1963 standard (he was IBM's alternate member). And, Bemer himself wrote: “it is not correct to say that I created ASCII.”

Design Decisions

Much of what makes ASCII look the way it does was decided by X3.2 between 1960 and 1962. And, unusually, the 1963 standard includes appendices that explain why.

7 Bits

The 1963 standard explains:

A 7-bit set is the minimum size that will meet the requirements for graphics and control… Both a 6-bit and an 8-bit set were considered and rejected — the 6-bit, providing only 64 graphics, could not accommodate essential format effectors, such as 'carriage return,' 'line feed,' 'horizontal tab,' etc; the 8-bit because it provides far more characters than are now needed in general applications.

The committee also wanted a code with no shifts — one where each character “shall stand by itself and not depend on surrounding characters.”

(It is often said that 7 bits were chosen to leave an 8th bit for parity. The standard does not give that reason.)

Control-Codes at the Ends

The all-zeros code (0x00) was reserved for NUL, and the all-ones code (0x7F) for DEL. DEL is all-ones for the same reason as Murray's “erase” character: on paper-tape, punching every hole over a character wipes it out.

So, the 64 printable (graphic) characters were put in the middle — 0x20 to 0x5F — and most of the control-codes were put in the first 32 positions.

Space First

Space (0x20) is the first printable character so that it sorts before everything else. The 1963 standard explains:

This permits a name such as 'Johns' to collate ahead of… 'Johnson.'

And, for the same reason, punctuation was put before the alphabet — so that “Johns, A” also sorts before “Johnson”.

Arabic-Numerals

The 1963 standard requires that:

The numerals 0 through 9 shall be so coded that the four low-order bits shall be the binary-coded-decimal form of the particular numeral.

This is why the least-significant nibble of each Arabic-numeral is its value. (See ASCII for the bit-tricks this allows.)

Keyboard Pairs

The 1963 standard also requires that characters which normally share a key on a typewriter differ in only one bit. For example, 2 (0x32) and " (0x22) differ only in bit-4.

Later, when lower-case letters were added, the same idea made each upper-case letter differ from its lower-case letter in only one bit (bit-5). But, that was not part of the 1963 standard — because the 1963 standard had no lower-case letters.

ASCII 1963

X3.2's code was named “American Standard Code for Information Interchange” in November 1961. Getting it approved took until 1963. (IBM, among others, voted against it.)

The ASA approved ASA X3.4-1963 on June 17th, 1963.

ASCII 1963 was quite different from the ASCII used today:

  • it had no lower-case letters — 0x60 to 0x7B were unassigned (reserved for future standardization),
  • it had ↑ (up-arrow) and ← (left-arrow) where ^ and _ are now,
  • it had no `, {, |, }, or ~,
  • ACK and ESC were near the end of the code (at 0x7C and 0x7E),
  • many of the control-codes had different names, or different meanings.

Revisions

ASCII was revised several times after 1963. Along the way, the organization that published it was renamed twice: the ASA became the USASI (United States of America Standards Institute) in 1966, and the USASI became the ANSI (American National Standards Institute) in October 1969.

X3.4-1986 is still the current ASCII standard. It has been reaffirmed several times since (most recently in 2022), and is now named INCITS 4-1986.

Lower-Case

Lower-case letters were left out of ASCII 1963 on purpose — there was disagreement over whether the last 2 columns of the code should be used for lower-case letters or for more control-codes. But, the 1963 layout had been designed so that lower-case letters could go there.

In October 1963, the ISO committee working on an international code voted to put lower-case letters in those columns. ASCII followed. The lower-case letters were in the (unpublished) 1965 edition, and first published in the 1967 edition.

Diacritical Marks

The 1967 edition made ^ a diacritical mark (circumflex accent). And, it allowed 5 other characters — ", ', ,, `, and ~ — to also be used as diacritical marks.

The idea was that, on a printer, a letter could be overstruck by a diacritical mark using backspace. I.e., “e, BS, '” would print “é”.

The 1986 edition deprecated this.

The Broken Bar

From 1967 to 1977, ASCII's vertical line (0x7C) was drawn as a broken bar (¦). This was a compromise with SHARE (an IBM users' group), who wanted a solid bar for the logical-OR operator. The committee instead allowed the exclamation mark (!) to be drawn as a solid bar (for logical-OR) — and drew 0x7C as a broken bar, so that the two would not be confused.

The 1977 edition restored the solid bar (|).

International Versions

ASCII was developed alongside international versions, and they influenced each other.

  • ECMA-6 — from the European Computer Manufacturers Association — first edition in 1965,
  • ISO/R 646 — an ISO Recommendation — in 1967,
  • ISO 646 — the first International Standard version — in 1973,
  • International Alphabet No. 5 — from the CCITT — in 1968 (today's ITU-T T.50).

These were not just ASCII with a different name. ISO 646 reserved a number of positions for national use — so that each country could put its own letters and symbols there. In ASCII, those positions hold characters such as @, [, \, ], {, |, }, and ~. For example:

  • the British version (BS 4730) has £ in place of #,
  • the German version (DIN 66003) has Ä, Ö, Ü, ä, ö, ü, and ß in place of [, \, ], {, |, }, and ~,
  • the Japanese version (JIS X 0201) has ¥ in place of \.

Adoption

IBM and EBCDIC

On April 7th, 1964, IBM announced the System/360 — one of the most important computer families ever made. It did not use ASCII. It used IBM's own 8-bit code: EBCDIC (Extended Binary Coded Decimal Interchange Code).

Bob Bemer later said that IBM's printers and card-punches were not ready for ASCII in time. Others have said that IBM's customers did not want to give up compatibility with their punched-card data.

(It is sometimes said that the System/360 had an “ASCII mode”. It had a bit that changed only the codes produced by its decimal-arithmetic instructions — and it used an IBM 8-bit variant that did not match the final ASCII standard.)

Because of System/360 and its successors, EBCDIC is still used on IBM mainframes today.

Teleprinters and Minicomputers

Meanwhile, the Teletype Model 33 was cheap, and became very common. Minicomputer makers (such as DEC, with its PDP computers) used it as a console and paper-tape device. And, time-sharing systems used it as a terminal. So, ASCII spread with it.

The US Government

On March 11th, 1968, US President Lyndon B. Johnson signed a memorandum approving ASCII as a Federal standard:

All computers and related equipment configurations brought into the Federal Government inventory on and after July 1, 1969, must have the capability to use the Standard Code for Information Interchange…

This became FIPS PUB 1 — the very first Federal Information Processing Standard — on November 1st, 1968.

Note that this required capability to use ASCII — not that ASCII be used for everything. Still, the US government was a very large buyer of computers. A later NIST history calls federal endorsement “the single most important reason for the subsequent wide adoption of ASCII.”

The Internet

On October 16th, 1969, Vint Cerf wrote RFC-20, “ASCII format for Network Interchange”, for the ARPANET (a predecessor of the Internet):

For concreteness, we suggest the use of standard 7-bit ASCII embedded in an 8 bit byte whose high order bit is always 0.

Many later Internet protocols were built on ASCII. For example, Telnet (RFC-854) uses “seven-bit USASCII in an eight-bit field”, and the original email protocol SMTP (RFC-821) carried only 7-bit ASCII. And, in email (MIME), text with no stated character-set is assumed to be US-ASCII.

In 2015, RFC-20 was made an official Internet Standard (STD 80).

Beyond 7 Bits

As 8-bit bytes became normal, ASCII's 7 bits left 128 more codes unused. Many character-sets filled them in — each keeping ASCII as its first 128 characters.

  • The IBM PC (announced August 12th, 1981) used CP437 (code page 437), which added box-drawing characters, accented letters, Greek letters, and more. One of its designers, David Bradley, said: “The IBM PC used the ASCII character set, a radical change for an IBM product.”
  • Commodore's PETSCII (from 1977) was based on ASCII 1963 — which is why it has ↑ and ← where ASCII today has ^ and _.
  • ISO 8859-1 (Latin-1, 1987) added letters for Western European languages.

These are sometimes called “extended ASCII”. But, there is no standard named “extended ASCII” — ASCII itself only ever had 128 characters.

Unicode and UTF-8

Unicode 1.0 was published in October 1991. Its first 128 characters (U+0000 to U+007F) are ASCII.

In September 1992, Ken Thompson designed UTF-8, with Rob Pike, at Bell Labs. Pike later wrote:

UTF-8 was designed, in front of my eyes, on a placemat in a New Jersey diner one night in September or so 1992.

A key property of UTF-8 is that ASCII characters are encoded as the exact same single bytes as in ASCII. As RFC-3629 puts it:

A direct consequence is that a plain ASCII string is also a valid UTF-8 string.

And, the bytes 0x00 to 0x7F never appear inside the encoding of any other character. So, software (and protocols) built for ASCII often work with UTF-8 without changes.

The Web

In December 2007, Google found that Unicode had overtaken ASCII as the most frequent encoding on the web pages it indexed. (Google counted a page as “ASCII” if it contained only ASCII characters.)

As of November 2026, W3Techs reports that 99.1% of websites use UTF-8, and less than 0.1% declare US-ASCII.

But, because UTF-8 contains ASCII, every one of those websites is still — in part — ASCII.
